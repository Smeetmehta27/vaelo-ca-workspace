A refinement of the existing mark — the serif wordmark, the monochrome ink-on-paper discipline, and the wordmark-first hierarchy all carry forward unchanged. What changes is the symbol, which becomes a deliberately constructed mark instead of a typographic aside, and the addition of one proprietary color for the contexts where monochrome alone can't carry recognition.

01 — The Mark

A V built the way the wordmark is drawn

The old "(V)" borrowed a punctuation mark. This one is constructed: two strokes of unequal weight converging to a single vertex — the same thick/thin contrast that gives the serif wordmark its character, translated into pure geometry. No chart, no currency mark, no literal finance symbol. The frame around it is a ledger cell, not a bracket — a quiet nod to the row-and-column discipline of the reports Vaelo actually produces.

  • Unequal strokes. The left arm carries the weight, the right arm is a hairline — the same optical contrast a serif V shows in the wordmark, so the icon and the letterform read as one hand.
  • Single vertex. Both strokes resolve to one point rather than a flat base — it reads as convergence, a single answer arrived at, not a container or a shape being filled in.
  • The frame is optional. At report and presentation scale it appears as a thin ledger-cell outline. At favicon and app-icon scale it's dropped entirely — the two strokes alone hold up on their own.
  • No literal finance iconography. No bar, no coin, no rupee mark, no arrow — the symbol earns its meaning from form and proportion, the same way the serif wordmark earns its authority from letterforms rather than a tagline.

04 — Proportion

Clear space, defined by the mark itself

The minimum clear space around the lockup equals the width of the thick left stroke of the V — a self-referential unit, so the system never depends on an arbitrary pixel value.

05 — Color

Monochrome first, one color to own

The report system stays ink-on-paper — that discipline is a genuine asset and isn't touched. One proprietary color is added for the contexts monochrome can't cover: product accents, social, and marketing. A muted bronze — closer to an old ledger stamp or a brass plate than to any fintech blue, teal, or purple — so it reads as financial and considered rather than trend-driven.
